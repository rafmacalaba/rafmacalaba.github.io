How often do World Bank projects use geospatial data?

Evidence from 2,261 projects, read through 4,494 Project Appraisal and Project Information Documents

In 6 in 10 World Bank projects that use data (1,165 of 1,932, 60%), teams draw on geospatial data, mostly at the level of regions and districts. How reliable is this?

  1. 2,261projects reviewed: every project with a PAD or PID in the collection
  2. 1,932use data in the passages read
  3. 1,165use geospatial data

Used in every sector and region

By approval year, the share runs from 59% (2021) to 62% (2023). 7 years of recent approvals are too few to show a trend.

Among the 111 countries with at least 5 projects, the share runs from 29% in Kyrgyz Republic to 100% in Guatemala.

What each level looks like in a document

Censuses and sector records carry the geography; big surveys are quoted as national totals

Uses of population censuses (78%), climate and weather data (62%) and infrastructure records (71%) are usually tied to a place.

Enterprise surveys (5%), Demographic and Health Surveys (15%) and poverty assessments (22%) are cited in many projects, but mostly as national figures, even though their data usually holds regional or district detail.

Maps, GIS and satellite data are almost always tied to a place (95%), but appear in fewer than 7 in 100 projects.

So whatThe fastest gain needs no new data: use the regional and district breakdowns already in the surveys teams cite.

What each level looks like in a document

Censuses and sector records carry the geography; big surveys are quoted as national totals

Uses of population censuses (79%), climate and weather data (61%) and agriculture and land data (64%) are usually tied to a place.

Enterprise surveys (5%), Demographic and Health Surveys (15%) and poverty assessments (22%) are cited in many documents, but mostly as national figures, even though their data usually holds regional or district detail.

Maps, GIS and satellite data are almost always tied to a place (95%), but appear in fewer than 3 in 100 documents.

So whatThe fastest gain needs no new data: use the regional and district breakdowns already in the surveys teams cite.

Frequently asked questions

What period does this sample cover?

The sampling period is January 2020 through September 2026. The World Bank project-list filter for this period shows 3,557 projects; PAD/PID documents were found for roughly 2,000 of them and enter this analysis.

Out of which projects?

Those that use data (the default) counts projects with at least one data mention judged used. All projects adds the 329 projects that mention data only as plans, bare figures or sources not relied on. Every one of the 2,261 projects in the collection has at least one reviewed passage, so this is every project, not a sample of them.

Projects or documents?

A project usually has more than one PAD or PID. Projects count a project once: it uses geospatial data if any of its reviewed documents does. Documents count each PAD or PID on its own. The project view is the reporting unit, because region, sector and approval year belong to the project. The project share is higher because a project has more text in which geospatial data can appear. The same document sometimes arrives from two collections under two ids; it is counted once, by its World Bank document id, and a passage read twice counts once. 4,708 confirmed document ids are 4,679 distinct documents.

What counts as geospatial data?

Data the document ties to a place: an exact location (a school, a gauge, a village), a named area (a region, district, camp or an urban/rural split; admin level 1 or 2 is enough), or a figure derived from maps or satellite imagery. Coordinates are not required. A topic such as agriculture or displacement does not count on its own.

What counts as using data?

Each data mention was judged for use: does the passage rely on existing data, or only name it, plan to collect it, or cite a bare figure? Only mentions judged as used count.

Why not count datasets?

The same dataset is named many ways (“DHS”, “2016 UDHS”, “Demographic and Health Survey”), so counts of distinct datasets are unreliable. Whether a project or document uses geospatial data is a clear yes or no. Source names are merged across spellings for display only.

How reliable are these figures?

Every number is counted from the records; none is typed in. The figures rest on AI-extracted data-use mentions from a sample of passages: an AI model found each data mention, judged whether the data was used, and labelled its geography against a written rubric, quoting the passage words behind each place label.

Every share is a floor. Each project was judged from a few sampled passages, not its full documents. A passage is an excerpt of a PAD or PID, about a page long, taken where the text mentions data; it is not a data mention, and one passage can hold several. 519 of the 1,932 data-using projects have only one passage read. The more we read, the more we find: of the data-using projects with one passage read, 39% use geospatial data; with five or more, 87% do. Projects with two or more documents reviewed reach 68%, against 43% with one document. Larger, data-heavy projects also had more passages read, so part of the rise reflects the projects themselves. Read in full, more projects would likely count.

PADs show more than PIDs. Where a PAD was reviewed, 70% of data-using projects use geospatial data (850 of 1,217); counting PADs alone, 62% of data-using documents do (680 of 1,092). A PAD is the full appraisal; PIDs are short and often written at concept stage.

Stricter checks. Under stricter rules the share falls. For projects that use data it is 60% without uncertain labels and 51% when the quoted evidence must also match word for word; for documents, 46% without uncertain labels and 37% when the quoted evidence must also match word for word. The word-for-word rule is harsh, because quotes taken from tables often reorder the cells. A defensible statement is between 51% and 60% of projects.

Coverage and limits

2,261 of 2,261 projects had passages reviewed, through 4,494 of their 4,679 distinct PADs and PIDs. 1,932 of the 1,932 data-using projects were approved in 2020 or later, so this describes recent practice and does not show a trend.
